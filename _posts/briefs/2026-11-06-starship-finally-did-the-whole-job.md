Starship is not a fireworks display, but rather a delivery van that finally learned to park.

On 26 August, the tenth test flight lifted off from Starbase, Texas, at 19:30 EDT. The Super Heavy booster lofted the ship, then aimed for the Gulf of Mexico. The ship itself cruised for about half an hour, then did something no Starship had done before: it opened its Pez-like dispenser and released eight dummy Starlink satellites into orbit.

Then came the hard part. Re-entry is where rockets go to die, and Starship's blazing plunge through the atmosphere tested a variety of new hexagonal heat-shield tiles. The point is the refurbishment the tiles are meant to avoid: a shield that survives re-entry with little to no repair is the difference between a rocket and a reusable lorry.

Officially, every major objective was met. Unofficially, the ship still ended its hour-long flight with a controlled splashdown in the Indian Ocean, then toppled over and exploded, as planned. After three previous failures, that counts as a triumph, not a tragedy.

NASA is watching closely, after all. Starship is its chosen Human Landing System for Artemis III, the first crewed Moon landing since Apollo, scheduled for 2027.

Conclusion: Starship is not a prototype, but rather a workhorse in training — tenth test, total triumph.
