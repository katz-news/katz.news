Ordering the ingredients of a bioweapon is about to get harder. Not impossible — harder.

As concerns mount that artificial intelligence could make creating dangerous pathogens even easier, US government-funded scientists who make and manipulate DNA sequences will face tougher regulations. The National Policy for Nucleic Acid Synthesis Screening, released on 5 November, will for the first time require these researchers to procure synthetic nucleic acids and benchtop synthesis equipment from US-approved suppliers. Those vendors must verify their customers' identities and screen orders for potentially dangerous sequences that, for example, could encode toxins or pathogens.

The policy's requirements will roll out in stages over the next 340 days, and it replaces a voluntary system of self-regulation. Some biosecurity experts view it as an important, if partial, step in the right direction. 'It's great, because it does something instead of nothing,' says Kevin Esvelt, a biotechnologist and biosecurity expert at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. 'It will make it harder to misuse DNA.'

Still, some key wording is vague, he and others say, and the policy only applies to federally funded scientists. But Esvelt believes it will spur synthetic biology companies to comply with the screening requirements for all customers — making it more difficult for 'competent lone wolves' to obtain synthetic DNA sequences for nefarious purposes.

Conclusion: the new rules are not a wall, but rather a filter — a fine filter, finely fitted, that catches the lone wolf.
