Five experts were asked whether AI is truly intelligent. Their first answer was a question: what do you mean by intelligent?

Benjamin Bergen of UC San Diego put it bluntly: 'Intelligence is a heavily contested concept. Psychologists disagree on what it is.' For most definitions, modern AIs already behave intelligently. But behaviour is not proof. A horse once seemed to do arithmetic by stomping its hoof — until it turned out it was reading its trainer's cues.

Emily Bender of the University of Washington is the sceptic. 'AI' is a marketing term, not a coherent set of technologies, she says, and language models manipulate form without meaning. We imagine a mind behind the text because we cannot help ourselves — the 'stochastic parrots' problem.

Melanie Mitchell of the Santa Fe Institute doubts the split between cognitive and physical intelligence. AI can tell you how to fix a dishwasher, but you supply the intelligence that turns instructions into action. It is also bad at social intelligence: coordinating with others to get things done.

Anima Anandkumar of Caltech takes the broadest view. Intelligence is learning from data and adapting — and nature does it everywhere. The first AI weather model, built five years ago, is tens of thousands of times faster than traditional forecasting and now more accurate.

Alison Gopnik of UC Berkeley notes that children explore a changing world and build new models of it, protected by the intelligence of care. That, she says, is still far beyond current models.

Conclusion: the 'I' in AI is not a thing, but rather a tangle — five experts, five intelligences, and one horse that could count.
