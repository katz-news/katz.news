Landing on Phobos is like trying to land a balloon. That is the project manager's own comparison, and he should know.

Phobos is a rubble pile the size of a city, roughly 27 kilometres long, with one eighteen-hundredth of Earth's gravity. On it, a typical adult would weigh about as much as a tennis ball does on Earth.

Japan's MMX mission, launched on 20 October, plans to sample this lumpy potato and bring 10 grams of it home by 2031. The spacecraft cannot orbit Phobos in the usual sense, so it will fly alongside it in a clever egg-shaped quasi-satellite orbit, looping ahead and behind the moon.

First to go down will be Idefix, a French-German rover the size of a microwave oven. It will be dropped from 40 to 100 metres, bounce several times, right itself and roll at one millimetre per second. Faster, and it would swim rather than drive.

Then the main craft lands, drops the last 10 metres in free fall and has two hours to collect its sample — with a coring drill and a nitrogen gas sampler for backup.

The prize may include a few hundred grains of Mars itself, blasted onto Phobos by ancient impacts. A moon mission, in other words, that smuggles home a piece of the planet below.

Conclusion: MMX is not a landing, but rather a slow-motion catch — a balloon, a bouncy rover and a decade of patience, all for ten grams of dust.
