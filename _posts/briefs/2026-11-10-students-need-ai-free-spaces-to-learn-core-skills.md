Let students argue with a machine, says one camp. Not yet, says this letter. Learn to think first — without the machine in the room.

Marc Schmitt, in his World view, has a hopeful plan: teach students to argue with artificial-intelligence systems, assess their debating skills, and let developers offer learning modes. Then, he argues, students will use such tools as a sparring partner rather than as an oracle. Concerns about losing critical-thinking skills are, he says, 'aimed at the wrong object': as with the calculator, abilities will 'come out sharper'.

Pablo Neira-Castro disagrees — politely, and at the root of it. The core skills in question are argument, debate and critical thinking. You cannot learn to argue by outsourcing the argument to the very tool that is supposed to teach you.

The calculator analogy, he says, misses the point. A calculator does arithmetic you already understand. A chatbot argues in your place before you have learned how.

The prescription is simple, and deliberately old-fashioned: protect spaces — classrooms, exams, exercises — where AI is absent, so students learn to think before they learn to prompt.

Conclusion: AI is not a sparring partner, but rather a shortcut — and shortcuts skip the very skills they promise to sharpen.
