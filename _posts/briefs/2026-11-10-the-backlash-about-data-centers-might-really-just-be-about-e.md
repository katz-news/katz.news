Big Tech insists the war on data centres is a culture war. It is not, but rather a power cut waiting to happen — literally.

James O'Donnell spent election week in Ashburn, Virginia — Data Center Alley, the world's largest data centre hub, and where the backlash arguably began. Driving through it is surreal: concrete monolithic buildings loom along every major road, named Skybox and CloudHQ.

Two days after the midterms, OpenAI's chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote that data centres 'have become a proxy for public unease about affordability, corporate power, and people losing control over their futures'. O'Donnell is not buying it. The backlash, he argues, is more about electricity than Big Tech admits — and no amount of public relations can escape the physical consequences of an enormous appetite for power.

There are two ways to feed an AI data centre: connect to the grid, or generate electricity onsite. In Loudoun County, Virginia, most data centres plug into the grid. The average one draws 54 megawatts — more than Denver International Airport. Activist Elena Schlossberg is 'always banging my head against the wall' when the industry calls it a culture war: 'It was always about power.' Protests have already forced new Virginia rules: data centres must pay fully for new transmission lines, and developers expect to be cut off from grid power during peak demand.

Elsewhere, the scale is too big for the grid anyway. The smallest data centres attractive for AI training draw 300 megawatts; the ideal ones, 6,000 — the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, powered by more than 20 power plants. So companies bring their own power: natural-gas turbines behind the meter, plus diesel generators. Anthropic has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines, the largest power generation purchase by an AI company. The turbines pollute; the diesel is worse; and in the rush, companies have become horrible neighbours — Elon Musk violated air quality laws running dozens of turbines at a Memphis data centre, according to the EPA.

The uncomfortable truth: there is only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Ask a small town to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant and the power plants to run them all, and trepidation is the rational response.

Conclusion: the backlash is not a culture war, but rather an electricity war — and the industry's ambitions are constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of power. The next frontier, Musk suggests, is space.
