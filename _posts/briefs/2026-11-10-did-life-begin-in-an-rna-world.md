Life may have started as a copy machine with no operator.

Four billion years ago, Earth was barren. Yet life took hold in nearly every corner of it. The question is how — and the leading candidate is a molecule that could copy itself. Chicken and egg first: in modern cells, DNA stores the instructions, RNA carries them, and proteins do the work. But proteins cannot be made without DNA, and DNA cannot be copied without proteins. Something had to come first.

In the late 1980s, scientists found the loophole. Some RNAs act as catalysts — ribozymes — speeding up chemical reactions the way enzymes do. RNA could therefore carry information and do the chemistry. Physicist-turned-biologist Walter Gilbert championed the idea: life began as an RNA world.

It is an elegant story with awkward gaps. In 2019, Gerald Joyce's team at the Salk Institute tried to evolve ribozymes in the lab and found mutations kept killing the catalytic behaviour. And the ribozymes were huge — several hundred nucleotides — while random assembly on the early Earth makes only scraps of 10 to 15 nucleotides.

But then came QT45. Philipp Holliger's team at the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology found a 45-nucleotide RNA, "quite tiny", that can seize another strand and copy it. In principle, it can copy any sequence. Two more such molecules turned up in a pool of ten trillion — a hit rate that suggests self-copying RNA might have been common.

The catch: copying is not living. Life also needs metabolism — a way to harvest energy. An RNA world would have been a copying machine, not yet a creature.

Conclusion: RNA may have been life's first draft, not its final version — a replicating, restless, remarkably resourceful beginning.
