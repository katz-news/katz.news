Five new science books, one shared verdict: the familiar world is stranger than it looks.

In Nature's Apothecary, Margaret Willes traces European herbalism from medieval times to the present, through 100 plants with medical properties. Hippocrates said nature itself is the best physician. The plants agree, from alecost to the daisy.

Sniff, by William Tullett, follows the human sense of smell from the 18th century onwards. When motor cars arrived, their emissions were first welcomed as a relief from horse dung. Smell, it turns out, is history you can inhale.

On Time, by Jim Al-Khalili, tackles time itself — from Einstein's claim that the distinction between past, present and future is only an illusion, to the question of whether the universe has a beginning or an end. Short chapters, big questions.

And in It Could Be Otherwise, Stuart Firestein defends science's oldest virtue: changing your mind. He shows how the great researchers resisted the temptation to twist facts to fit theories.

Conclusion: these books are not a reading list, but rather a reminder — a fresh, funny field guide to the familiar, from the garden to the nose to the clock.
