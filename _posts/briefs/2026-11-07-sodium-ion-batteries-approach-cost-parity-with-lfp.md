The battery made of table salt is about to get cheap. Sodium-ion batteries are approaching cost parity with lithium iron phosphate — the workhorse chemistry of the energy transition — according to Wood Mackenzie.

Leading makers should reach cell-level cost parity with LFP by late 2026, with more than 70 gigawatt-hours already covered by supply agreements in China, the United States and Europe. China is leading the charge: BYD plans 30 gigawatt-hours of sodium-ion capacity, and CATL aims to ship more than one gigawatt-hour by the end of 2026.

Up front, sodium-ion still costs more — about $229 per kilowatt-hour, against roughly $200 for LFP. Over a 20-year project, though, Wood Mackenzie reckons it could be 21 per cent cheaper to own, thanks to no capacity-expansion cycles and lower operating costs. Its wider operating temperature range also suits cold climates, where lithium-ion batteries need extra heating.

Policy is pushing the same way. China has exempted sodium-ion batteries from its lithium-ion consumption tax until the end of 2028, while American tariffs and tax credits encourage domestic production.

Conclusion: sodium-ion is not a lithium killer, but rather a salty, sturdy challenger — dearer to buy, cheaper to keep, and finally ready to compete.
