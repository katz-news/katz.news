Early-career teachers are leaving in droves. The fix might be a theatre game.

Nearly seven in ten early-career teachers leave the profession, or consider leaving, within their first five years, according to the Center for American Progress. Gabe Nitro — who only began teaching in 2025 — has a counterintuitive answer: improv.

After graduating from Stanford's Teacher Education Program, Nitro signed up for a second round of workshops. To his surprise, most of the activities centred on improvisational theatre. Making up stories and scenes in front of fellow teachers felt risky — and that was the point. Watching himself hesitate to engage after a mistake, he realised how much risk-taking learning demands, and how fear of embarrassment can stall a whole classroom.

He took the lesson into his science teaching. Instead of cold-calling for the 'right' answer, he now uses improv to make risk-taking the norm. In one exercise, students find someone with the same style of shoes and invent a handshake together — a small, silly risk that practises reaching out, experimenting, and accepting that an idea might be refused. The result: students who used to hide behind heads on desks or excessive loo trips came out of their shells, smiling and swapping ideas — and collaborated more readily in the science lesson that followed.

The research backs him up. A Yale study found student engagement is the driving force behind classroom environments that foster learning and achievement. As Marti Lane, who co-led the workshops, puts it: 'Learning itself is risky. It is vulnerable, it is scary, it is uncertain.'

Conclusion: teaching is not a performance, but rather a rehearsal — and in the rehearsal, a mistake is the lesson itself.
