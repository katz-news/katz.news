Australia's school results are not falling off a cliff. They are on a slow, steady slide — and the headlines missed the view.

PISA 2025, the world's largest education survey, tested 760,000 fifteen-year-olds across 91 countries, including 14,658 Australian students in 766 schools. The headlines screamed 'all-time low'. The numbers tell a calmer story: Australia is still above the OECD average in every subject, ranked 11th in reading and science and 17th in maths. Reading averaged 491 against an OECD average of 461; maths 478 against 463; science a stable 509 against 482. In the new digital problem-solving test, Australia scored 532, well above the OECD's 500.

None of that erases the decline. Reading and maths scores were lower than in 2022, continuing a slide since 2003 that is happening across the OECD — one in five fifteen-year-olds is now a low performer in all three domains.

Why? Nobody knows, and PISA does not say. Education Minister Jason Clare blames screen time; the OECD has suggested devices may have hurt reading. Both are guesses, and the author — who has researched PISA for 25 years — is blunt that we simply do not know enough yet.

The noise figure is real but old news: 41 per cent of Australian students report disruptive science lessons, against 31 per cent OECD-wide, much as in 2019 and 2022 — and self-reports are famously biased. The brighter figure barely made the news: 79 per cent say their teachers take an interest in everyone's learning, against 69 per cent OECD-wide.

Conclusion: the grades are not the story, but rather the starting point — and the starting point is better, and worse, than the headlines claim.
