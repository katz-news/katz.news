This sparkling ball of stars is nearly as old as the universe itself. It may also be a foreigner.

Messier 30, or M30, is a globular cluster some 27,500 light-years away in Capricornus, photographed by the Hubble Space Telescope. It is a roughly spherical swarm of tens of thousands to millions of stars, hanging in the Milky Way's halo like moths around a lightbulb. The Milky Way has at least 150 such clusters, and the oldest are 11.5 to 13.5 billion years old.

They are the galaxy's history books. A century ago, Harlow Shapley used variable stars inside globular clusters to prove the Sun is not at the centre of our galaxy at all — the centre lies towards Sagittarius. Globular clusters that formed with the Milky Way act as time capsules, preserving the chemistry of our young galaxy.

But M30 may not have formed here. It orbits in the opposite direction to the galaxy's spin, which suggests the Milky Way captured it long ago.

Hubble has watched M30 three times over 15 years, tracking how its stars move and how its populations differ. The new image combines all three visits, in ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light.

Conclusion: M30 is not just a pretty cluster, but rather a passport stamp — a sparkling, stolen souvenir from a galaxy our own swallowed long ago.
