Testosterone is having a moment — a loud, laddish moment. Fatherhood has the last word.

Darby Saxbe's new book, 'Dad Brain', arrives as testosterone is lauded on social media as the route to muscle, wealth and sexual dominance. Pete Hegseth has ordered testosterone screening for US service members over 30, in the name of 'restoring warfighter capability'. Online, men are told to 'Tmaxx' — to push their testosterone as high as it will go.

Saxbe, a clinical psychologist drawing on 20 years of research and her own brain-scan studies of men crossing into fatherhood, has bad news for the maxxers. Fatherhood naturally drops testosterone, and the more involved a man is, the more it drops. Testosterone is closer to a gauge than a lever: winning a contest pushes it up, losing pushes it down.

The largest review of the human research into testosterone, covering more than 15,000 people, found that a man's level barely predicts aggression — and giving a man extra testosterone makes no measurable difference to how aggressively he behaves. The World Health Organization trialled it as a male contraceptive: of 271 men given weekly injections, close to two thirds stopped producing sperm altogether, and among their partners there was a single pregnancy across more than a thousand months.

Across many species, male testosterone rises during territorial and mating competition and drops during the parental phase. Humans are no exception. Saxbe scanned 20 first-time fathers in Barcelona before their partners conceived and again two months after the birth, compared with 17 childless men: the fathers lost cortical grey matter volume while the deeper brain structures were spared. New fathers lost grey matter in the same regions that shrink in women's brains — a 'slimmed-down brain' may process information more efficiently.

Saxbe and Robin Edelstein followed 27 expectant couples and found fathers' testosterone declined, around 17 per cent lower at 36 weeks than at 12 weeks. The men whose levels fell furthest felt more invested and committed, and their partners described them as more supportive after birth.

Culture sets the ideal. Among the BaYaka, an egalitarian foraging people of the Congo Basin who prize touch and connection, the men rated the best dads have the lowest testosterone. Among the neighbouring Bandongo — a fishing and farming society anthropologist Lee Gettler describes as very patriarchal, very competitive and hierarchical — the best-rated dads have the highest testosterone. Same hormone, opposite ideal, set by what each society asks of its men.

Saxbe is careful not to flip the argument. In a study of 149 couples, fathers with lower testosterone at nine months postpartum reported more symptoms of depression, but their partners reported fewer; mothers whose partners had higher evening levels of testosterone reported more depressive symptoms at nine and 15 months postpartum and less satisfaction with the relationship. Her conclusion: interventions that supplement or alter men's testosterone may carry unintended consequences for the whole family.

Conclusion: testosterone does not make the man — and certainly not the father.
