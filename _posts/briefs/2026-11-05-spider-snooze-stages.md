Spiders sleep. That sounds obvious until you try to prove it — and now, for the first time, scientists have.

Researchers at the University of Bonn filmed the jumping spider Portia fimbriata through the night and found two distinct sleep stages: a quiet quiescence, and short bouts of twitching, leg curling and rapid eye movement — a REM-like state, the phase in which humans dream.

The proof is in the waking. Played a loud noise, awake spiders reacted in 1.69 seconds; quiescent ones took 2.71; REM-like ones 4.27. Slower to wake is the hallmark of sleep, and the REM-like spiders were slowest of all.

The lineages that led to spiders and to vertebrates diverged some 500 million years ago. If both sleep in stages, the architecture of sleep may be far older than we thought.

Conclusion: the spider is not a simple machine, but rather a sleeper with a structured night — and whether it dreams is a question we cannot yet ask it.
