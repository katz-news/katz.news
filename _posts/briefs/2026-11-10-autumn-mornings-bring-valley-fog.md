Fog is just a cloud that ran out of ambition.

On a cool November morning, low clouds traced the branching valleys of the Appalachian Plateau and the Cumberland River — then burned off by afternoon. NASA's Earth Observatory captured the vanishing act in a pair of images from November 8, 2026: the Terra satellite's MODIS sensor in the morning, and NOAA-21 VIIRS in the afternoon, after the fog had lifted and the rivers and valleys stood exposed.

The clouds appear to be fog, which is a cloud that forms near the ground. Foggy mornings are common here this time of year. As nights lengthen, the ground has more time to cool, and the air cools toward the dew point — the temperature at which water vapour begins condensing into tiny droplets. Cold air is denser than warm air, so it flows downslope and pools in valleys, exactly where the fog settles.

According to the National Weather Service, the region saw locally dense valley fog on the morning of November 8; earlier in the week, dense fog blanketed a wider part of the region and prompted advisories for hazardous travel conditions.

Conclusion: the fog is not weather going wrong, but rather physics going downhill — a cold, creeping curtain of autumn theatre.
