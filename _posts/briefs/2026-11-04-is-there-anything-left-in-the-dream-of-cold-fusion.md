Cold fusion is not dead, but rather just no longer about cold fusion.

The dream that erupted in 1989 — abundant energy, humanity saved — failed the headline test, but dig a little deeper and you will find a thriving research programme with exotic atoms that will not solve the energy crisis but stretches the boundaries of physics.

The real story is muon-catalysed fusion, first observed in the 1950s: swap the electron in an atom for its heavier sibling, the muon — or for kaons, pions or antiprotons — and the whole system collapses down. “That’s like a new periodic table,” says Daniel Swetz (an experimental physicist at NIST in Boulder). “You’re almost building it out in the third dimension.”

Shrinking atoms intensifies the electric field inside them — in kaonic fluorine, past the Schwinger limit, where quantum effects go berserk — and opens windows into fundamental physics, including the proton’s radius, measured in 2019. Even without reaching energy breakeven, muon-catalysed fusion could be useful (Shinji Okada, Chubu University); his team in spring 2026 used Swetz’s detectors to make the first direct measurement of X-rays from muonic hydrogen molecules, with the hydrogen as deuterium.

The lesson: do not judge a programme by its headline. Ask whether it will be interesting even if the main claim falls short — an all-or-nothing gamble on a single result, or a portal into a wider area of science? It is a lottery ticket, except the prize is not the jackpot, it is the shop (sorry, gamblers).

Conclusion: cold fusion is not an energy source, but rather a doorway.
