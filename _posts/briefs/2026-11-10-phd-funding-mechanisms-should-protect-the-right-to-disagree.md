Disagreement should not cost a PhD student their stipend. That is the argument of a new Nature correspondence — and it is hard to disagree with.

Nature recently reported what PhD students earn across Europe (Nature 658, 850–851). Nicolás C. Zanetta-Colombo asks an equally important question: what can they afford to refuse? Pay is one thing. The power to say no is another.

The point is about funding design. When funding follows favour, dissent dies. A doctoral student is often paid from a supervisor's grant, through a project the supervisor controls. Disagree with that supervisor — about the science, the direction, the working conditions — and the money is at risk. The right to disagree exists on paper. In practice, it costs a livelihood.

So the argument runs: funding mechanisms should be built to protect dissent. The student's stipend should not depend on one person's goodwill. If a student can afford to refuse, they can afford to think.

Conclusion: a PhD is not an apprenticeship, but rather an education — and education requires the freedom to say the supervisor is wrong.
