Your manicure smells like a chemical plant. For the people doing the manicures, it sometimes is one.

Researchers at the CUNY School of Public Health measured the air minute by minute for a week in five New York City nail salons. The typical level of volatile organic compounds was 15.1 parts per million. The peaks were another matter: the median 15-minute peak was 31.4 parts per million, and one salon hit 113. The lead researcher says the short-term peaks are comparable to levels in industrial settings where chemicals are manufactured.

The catch is the protection. Chemical plants have ventilation systems, monitoring and protective equipment. Nail salons often do not — and their workers, often women from underserved communities, breathe the fumes day in, day out. The main chemicals were ethyl acetate, acetone, n-butyl acetate and methyl acetate, with traces of benzene, which the researchers say could pose an elevated cancer risk with long-term exposure.

The surgical and cloth masks many workers wear are useless against the fumes. Ventilation is the real fix, along with open doors and windows, closed product containers and covered bins for chemical-soaked materials.

Conclusion: a nail salon is not a spa, but rather a small, smelly chemical workplace — and the air, it turns out, is the product nobody ordered.
