Congress does not need more lobbyists. It needs engineers who can explain things — plainly, precisely, in person.

San Antonio software engineer Nikhil Jathar first went to Washington in spring 2025, shortly after settling in the United States from India, for IEEE-USA's annual Congressional Visits Day. 'My first reaction was, "Wow, I get to visit Congress,"' says Jathar, CTO of AvanSaber, a business software company he cofounded. He went to advocate for federal support for technology education and research funding — and discovered Congress is not a fortress, but rather a calendar with very small windows.

Staffers expect clarity. A visit may last anywhere from 15 minutes to an hour, so Jathar kept his talking points simple and short: 'First and foremost you're gonna get a very small window. Make sure you've got everything ready to present in bullet points and not a lot of jargon.' One-page handouts help. And know what you are asking for: cosponsor a bill, change its text, add language to an appropriations bill, or ask an agency a specific oversight question.

The rules matter too. Congress distinguishes advocacy from professional lobbying, which is governed by the Lobbying Disclosure Act. On IEEE visits, participants represent the organisation, not their own views, and stick to its talking points — in Jathar's first year, funding smart grids, authorising Department of Energy research and investing in STEM education. 'STEM education funding is close to my heart,' he says. His calls were on the offices of Representatives Greg Casar and Lloyd Doggett of Texas and Barry Moore of Alabama. A staffer, he notes, beats the representative: staffers 'can be more focused on the policy details'.

One lesson was learned the hard way. On one visit, staff pointed out that their legislator was not on the committee with jurisdiction over the talking point. 'They explained that there are only certain things [the legislator] can do on that issue and that there are other people we need to talk to, to achieve more.'

Earlier this year, Jathar went back — and this time he brought what staffers actively want: authoritative technical knowledge. 'Do you think AI will replace coders?' one staffer asked. Jathar explained his company's open-source business-management software, ERPClaw, designed for AI agents such as OpenClaw, and the talk turned naturally to open source in AI, part of IEEE-USA's proposal for democratising AI.

'The people who build trust have more cachet with congressional staff because they've asked for something that's reasonably thought out,' says a House Science Committee staffer. His advice: 'you should feel emotionally connected to any issue you're advocating for.' Jathar is: open source, he says, lets smaller companies build on a shared foundation. 'We can progress faster. I'm not doing it only because it's good for my business, but because I feel it's good for any technological advancement.'

Conclusion: Capitol Hill is not a lobby, but rather a listening post — and engineers who arrive with a clear ask, a one-page handout and genuine conviction are the ones who get heard.
