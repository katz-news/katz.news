The robots do not eliminate sea lice. They manage them, one laser pulse at a time, and that distinction is the whole story.

Sea lice attach to farmed salmon and feed on their skin. The damage is tissue-deep: stress, wounds, impaired health across millions of fish in net pens. The standard cures are rough. Thermal delousing plunges fish briefly into heated water; mechanical removal strips lice off by force. Both injure the patient. For a typical cage of 150,000 fish, median mortality within two weeks reached 790 after thermal treatment and 928 after mechanical treatment. Norway's Veterinary Institute ranks post-delousing injuries as the single most important cause of salmon mortality. In 2024, about 57.8 million farmed salmon died during the seawater phase in Norway — 15.4% of the stock.

Stingray Marine Solutions, an Oslo company, offers a gentler weapon. Its underwater robots combine stereo cameras, blue-light illumination and precision lasers. The light sharpens the contrast between parasite and skin; the software tells louse from salmon; the laser kills the louse while the fish swims free. No handling, no hot water. Units fire up to five pulses per second and run around the clock. The AI here is high-speed image recognition guiding a target, not a chatbot with a ray gun.

The scale is real, and the numbers need attribution. Gadget Review cites Stingray at more than 2,000 robots protecting more than 50 million fish. Stingray's latest annual report puts it higher: 3,500+ units, 112 million fish monitored. A 2023 model with the University of Bergen found laser sites had about half the weekly probability of needing reactive treatment — roughly 5% against 10% at a 10°C reference. All figures are company-reported, not independently verified.

Two caveats, both documented. First, the lasers work best when lice pressure is low; under heavy infestation their effect is harder to observe. Second, the Veterinary Institute has recorded severe cataracts in harvest-size salmon at laser sites. Lasers spare fish the handling deaths, but they are not harmless.

Nor is the technology new. Optical delousing was patented in 2010 and has been in commercial use for roughly a decade. What is new is the viral moment: a post on 9 November, then the Gadget Review piece, then the world discovering that Norway's salmon farms are patrolled by robots. The headline says Norway deploys them. In fact a private company sells them to farms. The state merely counts the dead fish.

**Conclusion:** The laser is not a cure, but rather a trade — handling deaths for cataracts, and a decade-old tool for a problem that still kills one salmon in seven.
