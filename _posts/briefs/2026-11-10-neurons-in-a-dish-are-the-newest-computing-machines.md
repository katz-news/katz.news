Your next chatbot may run on brain cells. Literally.

Your favourite chatbot may one day run not on silicon microchips, but on real human neurons. Both systems use electricity to compute, transforming inputs into outputs — but neurons, for reasons nobody fully understands, are orders of magnitude more efficient than semiconductor chips. They need far less energy, fewer rare earth minerals, and a negligible amount of water for cooling. Their supply chains are less vulnerable too.

That is why some scientists think the data centre of the future might sit in a lab dish, using organoids — miniature models of the brain grown from stem cells — or other brain cell configurations. Last month, at the inaugural International Conference on Organoid Intelligence in Washington, D.C., researchers discussed that prospect and other ways to harness tiny tissue blobs for “biocomputing”: electrically stimulating neurons and interpreting their firing patterns through complex physical principles and algorithms.

The field has a paradox at its heart. Nobody fully understands how the brain actually works, in physical terms. Artificial neural networks are modelled on connections between neurons, but the analogy may say little about how real neurons compute.

“We can build something that is not really like the human brain at all, but still leverages some of the physical properties of neural networks to achieve something new,” says Brett Kagan, chief scientific officer at Cortical Labs.

Conclusion: the computer of the future is not a chip, but rather a culture — wetware, working wonders, one neuron at a time.
