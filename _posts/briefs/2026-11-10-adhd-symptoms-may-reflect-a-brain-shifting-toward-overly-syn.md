Your brain is a radio with a tuning problem. ADHD may be what happens when it drifts too far one way.

A healthy brain lives near a tipping point between chaos and order — too uncoordinated and signals get lost; too synchronised and every signal shouts at once. Researchers at the University of Helsinki hypothesised that ADHD symptoms reflect the brain tipping toward the overly synchronised side, and their results, published in Translational Psychiatry, largely agree.

They studied 70 adults — 36 neurotypical, 34 with an ADHD diagnosis — recording brain activity with magnetoencephalography during rest and two attention tasks. The ADHD group showed stronger long-lasting patterns in their brain rhythms: slower alpha waves during a detection task and faster gamma waves during a response task.

Crucially, the effect tracked symptoms across everyone, not just the diagnosed. In the gamma range, stronger patterns accompanied higher symptom scores. In the beta range the relationship curved: the strongest patterns appeared at moderate symptom scores, fading at both extremes — suggesting a little extra excitability tunes the brain up, while too much pushes it past the sweet spot.

There is a caveat, and the authors state it plainly. The study is correlational: it shows where ADHD brains sit, not why they sit there. The sample was modest, and an earlier resting-state study found nothing.

Conclusion: ADHD is not a broken brain, but rather a brain tuned slightly sharp — synchronised, striking, and still searching for its sweet spot.
