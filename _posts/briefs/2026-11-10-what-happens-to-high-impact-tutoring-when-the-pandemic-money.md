The pandemic money is gone. The tutoring it bought is not — yet.

Pandemic relief money paid for a big expansion of high-impact tutoring in schools. That money is gone, and districts now face a hard question: how to keep a tool proven to help students. Some have already cut back. Others have found ways to keep tutoring in place — but the school day is not getting any longer, and every change to a programme has a cost.

The danger, says David Blazar, associate professor of education policy at the University of Maryland, is not cancellation but dilution. He studied tutoring programmes in all 24 of Maryland's school districts, and the more likely outcome is weakened tutoring: bigger groups, fewer sessions, and sessions that stop helping students as much. He explains which features matter most, why Maryland districts described finding tutors as a major obstacle, and what his national look at tutoring found about how much students actually get.

Then the timetable problem. Devon Wible, vice president of teaching and learning at Catapult Learning, which provides tutoring programmes to schools, says conversations with districts have shifted from how fast tutoring can start to how it fits with classroom instruction. She walks through one third grader's day, explains why the tutor and the classroom teacher need to work from the same information, and shares the one move she would counsel teachers and principals to make this year.

Conclusion: tutoring is not dying, but rather thinning — and a thinner tutor is a tutor that stops working.
