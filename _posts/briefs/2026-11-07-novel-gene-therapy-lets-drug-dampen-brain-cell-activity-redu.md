Epilepsy can now be switched off with a pill. That is not a metaphor. It is a gene therapy.

For the first time, researchers have put drug-sensitive receptors into a human brain, so a drug can dampen the exact neurons that trigger seizures. A team in China treated three people with drug-resistant epilepsy, injecting a harmless virus carrying the receptor gene into the hippocampus, guided by live MRI. The gene switches on only in excitatory neurons — the ones driving the seizures — leaving inhibitory neurons untouched.

Afterwards, the patients took clozapine alongside their usual medication. Seizures fell by 92.6 per cent in the weeks after surgery, with only minor side effects. The alternative for these patients was surgery to remove the affected brain tissue.

The technique is called chemogenetics, and neuroscientists have used it in animals for twenty years. A decade ago, using it in humans was 'complete science fiction'. Nobody was willing to take the chance — until now.

The team has already treated fifteen or sixteen patients across epilepsy and pain trials, and has approval for phase 1 trials from the FDA and China's drug regulator. A Parkinson's trial is registered too.

Conclusion: chemogenetics is not a drug, but rather a remote control — a dimmer, a drug and a dose of daring, installed by virus and operated by pill.
