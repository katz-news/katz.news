That 'Alaska Native' walrus carving was probably made in Bali. Sorry, tourists.

Wildlife trade researcher Vincent Nijman first noticed something odd in a Bali workshop in 2013: ivory artworks depicting polar bears, salmon and cowboys — not elephant ivory, but walrus, and destined for the United States. After a 13-year investigation (reported in Biodiversity Conservation), he found widespread fraud: Indonesian carvings passed off as Alaska Native art. The numbers do not add up: between 2011 and 2024 the U.S. reported exporting 443 kilograms of walrus ivory to Indonesia, while Indonesia reported exporting more than 44,000 carvings back — Nijman estimates the true raw ivory entering Indonesia was four to 40 times greater than recorded.

The 1972 Marine Mammal Protection Act reserves walrus hunting and crafting to Alaska Natives; the fraud outsources their livelihoods to Bali and leaves buyers holding fakes.

Conclusion: the ivory is not Native, but rather a souvenir with a stolen signature.
