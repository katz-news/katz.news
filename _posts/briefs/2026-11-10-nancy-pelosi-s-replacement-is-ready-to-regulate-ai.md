Congress is about to get a lawmaker who treats AI risk like a fire alarm.

Scott Wiener won a tight race for Nancy Pelosi's open San Francisco seat, and he arrives in Washington with AI regulation as his signature issue. As a California state senator he authored SB 1047, the bill that would have been America's first real frontier AI legislation before Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed it in 2024. He followed it with SB 53, a lighter-touch version that Newsom signed. Both bills have inspired proposed federal legislation.

His interview with MIT Technology Review came hours after his victory was declared — and minutes after METR, an AI safety nonprofit, released a report widely read as evidence that Anthropic had achieved recursive self-improvement: AI meaningfully contributing to its own advancement. Wiener called it 'very concerning'. In twenty minutes he used the phrase 'recursive self-improvement' half a dozen times. 'We need to slow this down,' he said. 'Because it's moving very quickly, and we could sleepwalk into a disaster.'

His shopping list for Congress is specific: a select committee on AI, a strong and nimble federal regulator (an idea Representative Ro Khanna has proposed), whistleblower protections, testing and auditing requirements, and a kill switch. He is opposed to broad federal preemption of state AI laws — states, as Brandeis said, are laboratories, and Congress is not always nimble.

The awkward part is the money. Wiener's campaign took over $3 million from Public First Action, a pro-regulation nonprofit substantially funded by Anthropic. Critics call him too close to the industry. His answer: the group backed him because he was the first lawmaker to introduce AI regulation and get it implemented — 'shockingly cynical' to attack a candidate for regulating AI over Big Tech's objection.

Conclusion: Wiener is not heading to Congress to watch the AI debate, but rather to arm it — cautiously optimistic, and in a hurry.
