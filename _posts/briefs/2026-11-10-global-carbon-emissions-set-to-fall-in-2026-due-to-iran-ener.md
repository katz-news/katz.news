Global emissions are about to fall — for the worst possible reason.

Carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels are expected to drop this year, for the first time in five years. The Global Carbon Budget — put together by more than 140 researchers — puts 2026 at roughly 38.2 gigatonnes of CO2, a 0.4 per cent decrease on last year.

The cause is not a green breakthrough. It is a war. The US and Israel's war on Iran, which began in February, saw Iran blockade the Strait of Hormuz, the main trade route for about 20 per cent of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas. Oil prices jumped, and they are still high enough to hurt: petrol prices and household gas bills remain elevated, and countries have turned to alternative fuels to boost energy security.

The arithmetic is lopsided. Emissions from oil are projected to fall 1.9 per cent, partly offset by a 0.6 per cent rise in emissions from coal. Renewables helped too: electric vehicles, solar and wind are predicted to offset 64 to 79 per cent of the growth in emissions from rising demand through 2028. In China, the world's biggest emitter, emissions are projected to fall 0.9 per cent this year.

But the researchers warn this is a blip, not a breakthrough. CICERO's Robbie Andrew puts it plainly: with no further crises, you would expect emissions to bounce back. Corinne Le Quéré agrees: if oil prices rebound to pre-Iran levels, emissions will rebound too — until renewable energy is strong enough to bring them down durably.

Conclusion: emissions are not falling because we fixed the problem, but rather because a war made fuel expensive — a fossil-fuelled fall, and a fragile one.
