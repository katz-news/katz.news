Every app you use, every photo you take and every message you send comes down to billions of tiny decisions: yes or no, on or off, 1 or 0. The rules for combining those decisions were written by a schoolteacher in 19th-century England.

### The self-taught mathematician

George Boole was born in 1815 in Lincoln, England, the son of a shoemaker. His family could not afford university, so he taught himself languages and mathematics while working as a teacher. In 1854 he published *The Laws of Thought*, which showed that logical reasoning could be written as algebra. He became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork, Ireland.

### The idea: three simple words

Boole's algebra uses just two values — true and false — and a few operations:

- **AND** is true only if both inputs are true.
- **OR** is true if at least one input is true.
- **NOT** flips true to false, and false to true.

From these, you can build any logical statement, however complex.

### The leap to switches

For decades, Boolean algebra was a curiosity for logicians. Then, in 1937, a 21-year-old student named Claude Shannon wrote a master's thesis showing that electrical switches could carry out Boole's operations. Two switches in a row act like AND; two side by side act like OR. Circuits could now “reason.”

Today, a modern processor contains billions of transistors arranged into logic gates that do exactly what Boole described.

### Where it runs today

- **Every computer chip,** from phones to satellites, built from AND, OR and NOT gates.
- **Search engines and databases,** where you combine terms like “robots AND education NOT toys.”
- **Safety systems** in trains, aircraft and factories, where logic decides when to stop a machine.

### The practical lesson

Boole showed that very simple rules, combined patiently, can express almost anything. He also showed that you don't need privilege to change the world — only curiosity, discipline and good questions. A shoemaker's son with borrowed books gave us the grammar of the digital age.

### Try it yourself

Your lamp should turn on only if it is dark AND someone is in the room. Write that rule with AND, OR and NOT. Now add: “or if the alarm goes off.” You have just designed a logic circuit.
