Pick any even number bigger than 2. Now write it as the sum of two primes — numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves. 4 is 2 + 2. 10 is 3 + 7. 100 is 47 + 53. It always seems to work.

### A letter between friends

Christian Goldbach was born in Königsberg in 1690. He travelled widely, worked in St Petersburg and corresponded with the great mathematicians of his day — above all, Leonhard Euler. In a letter dated 7 June 1742, he suggested that whole numbers could always be written as sums of primes in a certain way. Euler replied with a cleaner version, the one we know today: every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes. Euler was confident it was true. He could not prove it. Nor has anyone since.

### Checked to the stars, proved to nobody

Computers have tested the conjecture for every even number up to 4 × 10¹⁸ — four billion billion — without a single failure. Yet in mathematics, evidence is not proof. A pattern can hold for a trillion cases and break on the next one.

There has been real progress. In 2013, the mathematician Harald Helfgott proved the “weak” Goldbach conjecture: every odd number greater than 5 is the sum of three primes. The original, “strong” version remains open.

### Why an unsolved problem is useful

Hard problems are training grounds. The search for a proof has pushed mathematicians to invent new ways of counting and estimating primes. Facts about primes also protect us every day. When you pay online or send a private message, your security depends on them — for example, that multiplying two large primes is easy, but splitting the result back into its primes is extremely hard.

### The practical lesson

Goldbach's conjecture shows the value of a good question. It is simple enough for a child to understand and deep enough to defeat experts for nearly 300 years. The best problems in science, engineering and business are like that: easy to state, hard to solve, and rewarding to chase — because of everything you learn along the way.

### Try it yourself

Write the even numbers from 4 to 50 and find two primes that add up to each one. Which numbers have the most ways? You are repeating, by hand, the first steps of a hunt that has lasted since 1742.
