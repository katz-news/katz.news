Some scientists change the world with a single discovery. Arnold Sommerfeld changed it by teaching.

### From Königsberg to Munich

Sommerfeld was born in Königsberg in 1868 and studied at its university, where Hilbert and Minkowski were also active. In 1906 he became professor of theoretical physics in Munich, where he built one of the most productive schools of physics in history.

### Sharpening the atom

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus only on certain allowed paths. It explained some of the light atoms give off, but not the fine details. In 1916, Sommerfeld extended the model with elliptical orbits and the effects of relativity. To describe the tiny splitting of spectral lines, he introduced a number now called the fine-structure constant, about 1/137, still one of the most important numbers in physics.

### Why metals conduct

In the late 1920s, Sommerfeld applied the new quantum mechanics to electrons in metals. His model explained why metals conduct electricity and heat so well. It was a key step toward the physics of semiconductors — the materials inside every transistor, chip and solar panel.

### The teacher of laureates

Sommerfeld was nominated for the Nobel Prize more than 80 times but never won. Yet several of his students and assistants did, including Werner Heisenberg, Wolfgang Pauli, Peter Debye and Hans Bethe. Few teachers have shaped a science so deeply.

### Where it runs today

- **Lasers,** which depend on the quantum energy levels Sommerfeld helped describe.
- **Electronics,** built on the quantum theory of electrons in solids.
- **Quantum technology,** developed by generations who learned from his students and their students.

### The practical lesson

The multiplier effect of teaching can exceed any single discovery. Sommerfeld gave students real problems, took their ideas seriously and connected them with each other. Communities of the competent and curious grow when experienced people invest in newcomers.

### Try it yourself

Think of one skill you have mastered. Who taught it to you, and who taught them? Trace the chain back as far as you can. Then ask: who could you teach next?
